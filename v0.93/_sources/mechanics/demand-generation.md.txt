# Demand Generation

Demand generation creates the pool of potential customers for each simulated travel day, or
[sample](counting-sims.md). It happens before sales and has two stages:

1. Generate total demand for each origin-destination market and passenger segment.
1. Allocate that total over booking timeframes and schedule customer arrival events before
   departure, using the segment's booking curve.

These are potential customers, not guaranteed bookings. When each arrival event occurs, the
customer's choice and the products then available determine whether a sale occurs.

The starting point is each demand record's `base_demand` in the initialized simulation, including
any scaling applied during initialization. Randomness controls both the variation in demand for one
record and the correlation between records in different markets or passenger segments.

(demand-generation-k-factors)=

## K-Factors to Control Correlation

Four k-factors perturb demand. Three share random draws across records to induce correlation, and
one adds independent variation:

| Setting                | Scope of the random draw                                       | Interpretation                                                                                   |
| ---------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| {{ sys_k_factor }}     | One draw for the entire sample                                 | A high-demand day tends to increase demand across the system.                                    |
| {{ mkt_k_factor }}     | One draw per origin-destination market, shared by its segments | Business and leisure demand in the same market tend to rise or fall together.                    |
| {{ segment_k_factor }} | One draw per passenger segment, shared across markets          | Business demand, for example, can rise across markets without the same change in leisure demand. |
| {{ simple_k_factor }}  | An independent draw for each demand record                     | Adds variation specific to a market and passenger segment.                                       |

Each draw is a standard normal random variable, with mean zero and standard deviation one. Shared
draws are reused within a sample; new draws are made for the next sample.

For a base demand $b_{ods}$ in origin-destination market $od$ and segment $s$, the perturbed mean is

$$
\mu_{ods} = \max\left(0,\ b_{ods}\left[
1 + k_{\mathrm{sys}}R_{\mathrm{sys}}
+ k_{\mathrm{mkt}}R_{od}
+ k_{\mathrm{segment}}R_s
+ k_{\mathrm{simple}}R_{ods}
\right]\right).
$$

The k-factor terms are **relative** perturbations: their sum scales base demand rather than adding a
fixed number of customers. Larger factors increase variability. Setting a factor to zero removes its
contribution. The perturbed mean is clipped at zero so the next stage has a nonnegative mean.

The old `pax_type_k_factor` name is a deprecated alias for `simple_k_factor`. It refers to
independent variation, not the cross-market correlation controlled by `segment_k_factor`. Keeping
that distinction is important when translating older configurations or PODS terminology.

```{note}
The equation above describes the usual setting `simple_cv100=0`. The experimental
`simple_cv100` option, when positive, replaces the independent perturbation and takes precedence
over `simple_k_factor`.
```

(demand-generation-z-factor)=

## Z-Factor to Control Variance

After constructing the perturbed mean, PassengerSim makes an additional independent normal draw for
each demand record. Let $z$ be
[`tot_z_factor`](passengersim.config.SimulationSettings.tot_z_factor). The standard deviation and
sample demand are

$$
\sigma_{ods} = \sqrt{\mu_{ods} z},
\qquad
D_{ods} = \max\left(0,\ \mu_{ods} + \sigma_{ods}R_{\mathrm{residual}}\right).
$$

Here $R_{\mathrm{residual}}$ is another standard normal random variable. Before clipping, the
conditional variance is $\mu_{ods}z$, so the z-factor is **variance divided by mean**, not its
reciprocal. This is the variance of the final draw conditional on the perturbed mean; k-factors also
contribute to variation across samples.

Negative results are clipped to zero. Clipping either the perturbed mean or the final draw can make
average generated demand higher than the original base demand. A zero perturbed mean produces zero
demand, and `tot_z_factor=0` removes this final random contribution while retaining any k-factor
variability.

For example, if base demand is 100, the four k-factors are 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, and 0.4, and all four
k-factor draws happen to be 1, the perturbed mean is 200. With `tot_z_factor=2`, the standard
deviation is 20. A residual draw of 1 then gives sample demand of 220.

A demand record configured with `deterministic: true` bypasses both stages of total-demand
randomness and uses its base demand directly. This fixes its total, but does not by itself make
customer arrival times deterministic.

(demand-generation-timeframes)=

## Demand Allocation to Time Periods

The generated `scenario_demand` is rounded to a nonnegative integer passenger count using
`int(scenario_demand + 0.5)`: half passengers round up. This count is then allocated over booking
timeframes using the booking curve and
[`tf_k_factor`](passengersim.config.SimulationSettings.tf_k_factor), which controls variability in
the timing of demand rather than the total count.

[`timeframe_demand_allocation`](passengersim.config.SimulationSettings.timeframe_demand_allocation)
selects the allocator: `"pods"` uses the PODS-style allocation, while `"v2"` uses the alternative
allocator. Both must preserve the rounded total; PassengerSim checks that the number of allocated
events equals that count and raises an error if it does not.

Separating total demand from arrival timing lets a scenario vary the size of the customer pool, the
correlation between markets and segments, and the timing of customer arrivals independently through
their respective controls.
